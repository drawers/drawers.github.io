Split the difference

Splitting the difference between two opposing views often seems clever. “Those darn’d hardliners! I’m a centrist!”

On software teams, there are often disputes between those who want a quality solution and those who just want to ship. For a manager here, splitting the difference between the two can seem like a great option. Maintaining this stance makes one seem a pragmatist in a sea of idealists. Since neither party gets their way, it can allow a manager to seem fair and impartial.

It’s wrong to split the difference when low quality work is slowing down the team. This is not always the obvious, like in the case of time spent patching broken features in production. It can also manifest in back-to-dev-from-test cycles and last minute tactical changes towards the end of a project. Or finding that features are difficult to adapt when revisited a few months later.

While hard data here is tricky, on a small team anecdotes may be enough to get a handle on what’s really going on. In my experience, very few teams have engineering standards that are too high. It’s usually the other way around, just no one noticed yet. No one noticed, perhaps, because they were too busy splitting the difference.
